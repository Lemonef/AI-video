# Eiffel Ink — full script (target 13 min, ~2000 words at ~150 wpm)
Hook rule: contradiction + stakes in first 10 s; every act ends on an open loop; a pattern interrupt every ~45 s.

## ACT 0 — HOOK (0:00-0:40)  open loops: L1 "how", L2 "the one mistake", L3 "Capone"
b01 [paris_dusk] In nineteen twenty-five, a man sold the Eiffel Tower. Not a model. Not a ticket. The actual tower.
b02 [paris_tower_grey] He sold it twice. And he got away with it.
b03 [paris_night] Then he conned Al Capone, escaped a federal jail, and was sent to Alcatraz.
b04 [hotel_room_night] His name was Victor Lustig. And his whole trick fit inside one sentence.
b05 [paris_dusk] By the end of this video, you will know the sentence. And you will understand why it works on almost everyone.

## ACT 1 — THE TOWER WAS NEVER MEANT TO STAY (0:40-2:30)  loop: "why would anyone believe it"
b06 Start with the tower itself. It was built in eighteen eighty-nine, for a world's fair.
b07 It was supposed to stand for twenty years. Then be taken apart.
b08 Most Parisians hated it. Artists signed a petition calling it a gigantic black factory chimney.
b09 It only survived because the army found a use for it. A giant radio antenna.
b10 So by nineteen twenty-five, the idea of the tower being temporary was already in the air.
b11 And the tower was in bad shape. It needed paint, it needed repairs, and the bills kept growing.
b12 Newspapers asked the same question every week. Is this thing still worth keeping?
b13 Most people just read that, and moved on.
b14 Victor Lustig read it, and saw a door open.
x01 [paris_grey] Think about what he saw. Not a monument. A pile of iron, in the middle of a city, that people openly argued about keeping.
x02 [paris_grey] Every con needs a believable story. The tower was already telling one for him.
x03 [paris_tower_grey] All he had to do was finish the sentence the newspapers had started.

## ACT 2 — WHO LUSTIG WAS (2:30-4:15)  loop: "he's good, but how good"
b15 He was born in eighteen ninety, in a small town in Bohemia.
b16 He spoke five languages. He looked like a diplomat. He never raised his voice.
b17 He had no gun and no gang. Just a suit, a smile, and a lot of patience.
x04 [hotel_room_night] His real name was not even Lustig. He used more than forty names, and carried passports to match.
x05 [paris_night] Police in several countries had files on him. None of the files agreed on who he was.
x06 [paris_dusk] That mattered. A man nobody can pin down is a man nobody can chase.
b18 He worked the ocean liners between Europe and America, because rich people on a ship have time to talk.
b19 He even had rules. Ten of them. He wrote them down, like a business manual for lying.
b20 The first one: be a patient listener. Let the other person talk, and tell you what they want.
b21 Number two: never look bored. Number three: wait for the other person to reveal their politics, then agree with them.
x07 [hotel_room_night] Number four: never boast. Number five: never be shabby. A rich person trusts clean shoes.
x08 [hotel_room_night] Notice what is missing. Not one rule says be clever. They all say be careful, be calm, be kind.
x09 [paris_grey] The best con men are not the loudest people in the room. They are the ones you are glad to meet.
b22 We will come back to those. Because on one afternoon in Paris, he used almost all of them.
b23 He had the idea. Now he needed a costume, a stage, and an audience.

## ACT 3 — BUILDING THE LIE (4:15-6:15)  loop: "will the papers hold"
b24 First, the costume. He forged letterhead from the French government.
b25 Then he gave himself a title. Deputy Director General of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs.
b26 Why that ministry? Because the tower already carried radio equipment. It sounded real.
b27 Then the stage. The Hotel de Crillon, one of the most expensive hotels in Paris.
b28 Not a back room. Not a warehouse. A palace, with doormen, and chandeliers.
b29 He understood something simple. People trust the room before they trust the person.
b30 Then the audience. He did not want rich men. He wanted men who wanted to be richer.
b31 Six scrap metal dealers. Each one invited by a polite, official letter.
b32 The letter said only this. Come to the Crillon. Discuss a matter of great importance. Tell no one.
b33 The dealers arrived thinking they had been picked. That was the first hook.
b34 Lustig knew the smallest detail could ruin him. One wrong word, and six men could walk out.
x10 [hotel_room_night] So he prepared every detail. The papers, the title, the hotel, the order he would speak in.
x11 [paris_night] One slip, and six men could walk out and call the police.
x12 [paris_dusk] There was no second take. The whole plan lived or died in one afternoon.

## ACT 4 — THE PITCH (6:15-8:30)  loop: "who bites"
b35 He closed the door, and spoke quietly. Gentlemen, what I tell you is a state secret.
b36 The Eiffel Tower is too expensive to maintain. The government has decided to demolish it.
b37 But it cannot say so. The public would revolt. So the sale must be kept confidential.
b38 Seven thousand tons of iron. A fortune, for the right buyer.
b39 To prove it, he took them in two limousines, to see the tower in person.
b40 They walked around it, looking up at it differently. Not as a monument. As inventory.
x13 [paris_tower_grey] He did not rush them. He let them see the tower, up close, and imagine the iron already theirs.
x14 [paris_tower_grey] Because the moment a buyer pictures owning the thing, he has already started to pay for it.
b41 Lustig watched them carefully. And he picked his target.
b42 Andre Poisson. A businessman with money, but without a place in the Parisian elite.
b43 Poisson wanted to be taken seriously. And Lustig gave him exactly that feeling.
b44 He hinted that the contract would go to the highest bidder. Poisson understood. He thought he was being smart.
b45 Then the second hook. Poisson's wife grew suspicious. This all felt a little too fast.
b46 Lustig did not argue. He shifted the story.
b47 He admitted, with embarrassment, that a low paid civil servant needs a little extra to survive.
b48 Poisson relaxed. A bribe made the whole thing feel real. Corruption is something people already believe in.
b49 That is the genius. Lustig did not ask him to believe a lie. He asked him to believe in a small sin.
x15 [hotel_room_night] And there was a second reason it worked. Poisson was new to the city's business elite. Everyone else had a name. He wanted one.
x16 [paris_night] Lustig did not sell him a tower. He sold him a seat at the table.

## ACT 5 — THE VANISH (8:30-10:15)  loop: "why no police"
b50 Poisson paid for the tower. And paid the bribe on top.
b51 Lustig took the cash, took the train, and left the country, to Vienna.
b52 He bought newspapers every morning, and waited. Nothing.
b53 No headline. No arrests. No manhunt. Silence.
b54 Because Poisson had done something he could not undo. He had paid a bribe, to buy a national monument.
b55 If he went to the police, he would be the man who tried to bribe a minister, and lost.
b56 Shame did the work that Lustig no longer had to do.
x17 [hotel_room_night] Meanwhile Poisson waited for the deal to move, believing he was part of a secret.
x18 [paris_tower_grey] Every time he looked at the tower, he saw something he thought was his.
x19 [paris_night] The tower did not move. Weeks passed. And slowly, a different feeling started to grow.
b57 So, a few months later, Lustig came back to Paris, and did it again.
b58 Another set of dealers, the same hotel, the same script.
b59 This time, one of them went to the police.
b60 Lustig left France that night. He did not even stay to count his luck.
x20 [paris_grey] Here is the part most retellings skip. He had already won. He had a fortune, and a clean exit.
x21 [paris_night] But a con man who stops is just a thief with a hobby. Lustig needed the story to keep going.
x22 [paris_dusk] That is what finally caught up with him. Not the police. The need to be believed again.

## ACT 6 — AMERICA AND CAPONE (10:15-12:00)  loop: "can he still win"
b61 He crossed the Atlantic, and found a bigger stage. America, in the prohibition years.
b62 He built a little wooden box, and told buyers it printed perfect hundred dollar bills.
b63 He sold it for tens of thousands of dollars. The box did not print anything.
x23 [paris_grey] The trick of the box was simple. Two real bills went in. Six hours later, two bills came out. Perfect copies.
x24 [paris_night] Of course, the extra bills were also real. Hidden in the box. The buyer saw a miracle, and paid for a miracle.
x25 [hotel_room_night] A sheriff in Texas paid thirty thousand dollars for one. By the time he understood, Lustig was long gone.
b64 Then came the boldest move. Al Capone.
b65 He told Capone he needed fifty thousand dollars for an investment, and promised to double it in sixty days.
b66 Capone gave him the money, and a warning. Do not make me regret it.
b67 Lustig put it in a safe deposit box, and did nothing with it. For two months.
b68 Then he walked back, with a sad face. The deal fell through, sorry.
b69 He returned every dollar. Capone was so impressed by his honesty that he gave him five thousand.
x26 [hotel_room_night] Think about what Capone saw. A man with money in his hands, and he gave it all back.
x27 [paris_night] To Capone, that was not weakness. That was proof. In a world of liars, here was the one honest man.
b70 That was the whole point. The money was never the con. The trust was.

## ACT 7 — THE FALL (12:00-13:15)  loop closes: the sentence
b71 In nineteen thirty-five, his luck ended. The Secret Service caught him, with counterfeit money.

b74 His death certificate lists his occupation as apprentice salesman.
x28 [paris_grey] The man who had sold the most famous building in the world, died as an apprentice.
x29 [paris_dusk] No money. No title. Just a tidy number on a form.
b75 So what was the sentence?
b76 Give people a reason to feel special, and they will help you fool them.
b77 The tower never moved. The man who sold it only borrowed a little of our oldest weakness.
b78 If this story surprised you, tell me which part of the con you would have fallen for.
